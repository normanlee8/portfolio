Overview

Users
Recruiters first, then me, then anyone who lands here. A recruiter might give a portfolio a minute or less, on a phone or a work laptop.
Challenge
I wanted project covers that move and react, because they make the site feel like mine. But effects like those can make a site load slowly or stutter, especially on an older or locked-down work computer, and someone with a minute shouldn't spend it waiting.
Goal
Get visitors to the information they need as quickly as possible, on any device, while keeping the small details that make the site feel like me.
Solution
A site I designed in Figma and built in plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, then measured and adjusted until scrolling stayed smooth, including on a computer without graphics acceleration.
Constraints
Live by the end of October, so I could focus on the projects that go in it. No frameworks. Viewable on every device: phones, tablets, and computers.

Who It's For

Recruiters, me, and anyone who ends up here.

With recruiters in mind, the idea behind every page is to get the viewer to the necessary information as quickly as possible: who I am, what I've made, and how to reach me. I balanced that with small touches I find cool, like the page fade-in and the line under the header that fills as you scroll, the kind of details that can go unnoticed.

  • Works everywhere

    Phones, tablets, and computers. I designed every page at three sizes so it's viewable no matter what.

  • Feels quick

    Pages load and switch quickly, and scrolling stays smooth, including on slower computers.

  • Feels like me

    Simple, clean, organized, and empathetic. Over-the-top visuals aren't who I am; I like the little things instead, like the location arrow, the bouncing arrow on the landing page, and "User Experience Designer" fading away as you scroll.

Keeping the Moving Covers

The most fun part of the site was also the slowest.

The project covers are the most personal part of the home page. The portfolio cover is a tilted wall of the site's own sections that starts scrolling when pointed at, and the Apple Sports cover lifts parts of a phone screen off the page. Each is made of many pieces, and on a computer without graphics acceleration every piece was redrawn on every frame of scrolling, so the page stuttered right where the work starts.

I didn't want to lose them, so I changed when they're live. At rest, each cover is now a single picture of itself, rendered from the live cover so the two look the same. The live cover only switches on when someone points at or tabs to the project, and its images load ahead of time so the switch is hard to notice.

Images
About 540 KB in 23 files before; about 170 KB in two now, on a typical screen.
Scrolling
Without graphics acceleration, 11 to 15% of frames were dropped past the covers before; under 1% now.

Trade-offSwapping the covers for plain screenshots would have been fast with no extra work. Keeping them costs more upkeep: the pictures have to be rendered again whenever a cover changes, so the site includes a small script that renders every size and theme in one step.

Motion Where It Counts

Keeping the motion that adds something, and trimming what slowed the site down.

Moving between pages, the site used to slide the Apple Sports cover across the screen into the next page. It looked nice the first time, but on every page change it made the site feel slower, so pages now fade in, in a third of a second. A fade that sharpened out of a blur looked softer, but blurring the whole screen on every frame cost 10 to 30 dropped frames on each page load, even with a graphics card, right when a visitor starts to scroll. It's a plain fade.

The home page is the one exception. Its parts still rise in one after another, because it's the first impression, but faster than before: done in 1.5 seconds instead of 2.6. The theme switch went the same way: instant felt abrupt and a slow cross-fade felt unresponsive, so it fades in a fifth of a second.

Kept

  • The home page's entrance, quicker
  • A short fade between pages
  • Covers that come alive when pointed at

Cut

  • The cover flying between pages
  • The blur in the page fade
  • Waiting on images before switching themes

Designing for Slower Computers

Testing on a slow setup, not just my own computer.

I tested the site with graphics acceleration turned off, as a stand-in for an older or locked-down work laptop, on the idea that if it holds up there, faster computers will be fine. Without graphics acceleration, the browser draws everything on the processor, and effects like the header's frosted glass are worked out again on every frame of scrolling. On those computers the site switches to a lighter look: the glass becomes a nearly solid tint of the same color, which looks almost the same. It remembers the choice, so later visits start that way from the first frame.

Measuring also turned up work that wasn't visible at all. On the case study pages, a few style rules and the reading-progress counter made the browser restyle the whole page on nearly every frame of scrolling. Rewriting them cut that work from 482 ms to 14 ms over the same scroll, with nothing changing on screen.

Trade-offA few effects look slightly different on slow computers, so they stay smooth. I'd rather a recruiter sees a nearly solid header than a stuttering one.

Early Concepts

From a first sketch, to an experiment, and back to clean.

I knew from the start that I wanted a streamlined, minimal portfolio that gets straight to the point. My first two designs were that first sketch, and they're nearly the same: the only change moved the navigation links from the hero into the header.

It felt too basic, so I experimented with lined paper. Loose-leaf paper works on a light or a dark background, and I already knew I wanted a theme toggle. It's something nearly everyone has used, it looked unique, and it's academic, organized, and to the point, much like the years of school I spent with it. For the next version, I set the page on my family's wooden table, which has been in our home for as long as I can remember and is a fixture in many Asian households. To add a little realism, the navigation links became sticky-note tabs, the way they'd mark pages in real life.

I didn't go with it. There was too much going on: the table, the paper on top of it, the different-colored tabs, and lines that made text hard to fit. It also felt too gimmicky. Lined paper is much taller than it is wide, so stretching it across a wide screen didn't look right, which is why the table filled the sides.

Next, I tried a blue gradient with project cards to see how it would look, and I liked it. It was still basic, but it was clean, which is something I really like. Blue is my favorite color, and it conveys trust. The second blue version used a gradient that's brighter at the top and darker at the bottom, like a sky, moved the project cards down, and let the landing page fill the whole screen. From there, a few more rounds led to the final designs.

Easy on the Eyes

A warm off-white and a soft gray, in light and dark.

In the end I dropped the blue, because it isn't neutral. Not everyone likes blue, or green, or any single color, so I'd rather the site be a little "boring" and inoffensive, in off-white and gray, and let the work stand out. I care about accessibility: everyone should be able to use the site comfortably.

People also have preferences. I know plenty of people who prefer dark mode, plenty who prefer light, and some who like to switch, so the site has both. It opens in whichever theme the device is set to, and a button in the header switches it.

Neither theme is pure white or pure black. Pure white is harsh on the eyes, and True Tone and Night Shift warm a screen slightly to reduce eye strain, so the light theme is slightly warm too, without going so far that it looks yellowed. The dark theme is a gray I chose by eye: not so light that it barely makes a difference, and not so dark that it's hard to look at, since pure black can be harsh too.

Choosing the Words

A clearer title, and a shorter headline.

I changed my title from Product Designer to User Experience Designer. "Product designer" felt vague, and I had nothing yet to back up the "product" part. What I care about most is making people's lives easier. Nice interactions, interfaces, and visuals matter to me too, but the user's experience comes first.

For the headline, I chose "Make life easier." over "Designing paths of least resistance." They say nearly the same thing, but "Make life easier." is punchier and to the point, while "paths of least resistance" can be confusing.

A Small Mark

The icon in the browser tab, after 26 tries.

The favicon is the small icon in the browser tab and in bookmarks, so it has to read at just a few pixels wide. I tried 26 designs: my initials, NL, in blue; my name spelled out, on one line and stacked; a single bold N, some cropped to fill the whole square; and Ns with layered, stepped shadows, upright and turned at an angle.

From Figma to Code

Designed at three sizes, then built component by component.

I designed every page at three widths: 393px for phones, 768px for tablets, and 1440px for computers. I laid each frame out with absolute positioning first, which gave me full control to see how every element looked next to the others. Then I rebuilt each one with auto layout, which works much like CSS flexbox, the part of CSS I knew best, so I could turn the designs into code almost one to one.

I built the site myself instead of using Framer, Webflow, or Squarespace because the Figma-to-HTML, CSS, and JavaScript pipeline is the one I know, so there was almost no learning curve, and building it would teach me more along the way. I opened VS Code with three files, one each for HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and worked page by page, going through each component in Figma and coding it. I skipped frameworks like Tailwind: they're another thing to learn, and plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript was the fastest way for me to get it running.

In code, the breakpoints moved. The desktop layout switched at 1280px instead of 1440px, and a new breakpoint appeared at 1024px, because the gap between the tablet and desktop designs was too big. A layout that looked right at 768px looked odd at 1280px, where a different design fit better.

In hindsightI didn't have the foresight to plan for those in-between sizes in Figma, and it's the part of my process I'd most like to improve. Designing for more widths up front would have saved reworking layouts in code.

Small Things, Less Effort

The details I'm proudest of: shortcuts that make the visitor's life easier.

  • Home from my name

    Hovering my name in the header shows a small arrow, a hint that it leads home.

  • Projects from the menu

    Hovering Work opens a list of projects, so visitors can go straight to one without stopping at the Work page.

  • Previous and next

    The end of each project links to the next one, so there's no trip back to the Work page.

  • On this page

    A list of sections jumps anywhere in a case study and shows where you are: at the side on wide screens, in the bottom bar on smaller ones.

  • Return to top

    One click back to the top of a long page.

  • Theme toggle

    Light or dark, starting from the device's own setting.

It's all about making the visitor's life easier, and I kept every one of these while keeping the site looking clean.

Building with Claude Code

I designed it; Claude Code helped me build it faster.

I coded the first version by hand. As the site grew, so did the complexity, and some of it would have taken months or even years to learn properly, with a deadline to have the site live. I switched to Claude Code to put changes in place much faster.

I'm positioning myself as a designer, not a design engineer or a full-stack developer: a designer with enough front-end knowledge to get a site up and running. I made the design decisions, and Claude Code wrote the code faster. I reviewed every change it made, and nothing went in without my supervision. Claude Code did suggest ideas, mostly small stylistic tweaks, but about 90% of the site is my own ideas. The performance work above was done the same way: Claude Code measured the site and proposed changes, and I decided what to keep.

I also said no often. I turned down two redesigns of the Work page, several takes on the header's glass blur, and a theme switch that spread out from the button in a circle.

Next Steps

More case studies, and steady refinement.

There's nothing to measure yet: the site only went live recently, Google only recently started showing it, and I haven't applied anywhere with it. Next, I'll add case studies as I finish them, starting with a refresh of the San Francisco Public Library website, and keep adding the small improvements I think of along the way.

What I Learned

Plan for every screen size from the start, not just three.

A simple, neutral site can still feel personal. The personality lives in the small details, not in the colors.
