Overview

Context
A portfolio is often the first thing a recruiter sees, and most only have a minute or two to look.
Problem
Many portfolio sites I came across lag, load slowly, or put visuals ahead of the work. I wanted one that gets visitors to what matters quickly and still feels like me.
Why it matters
If a site is slow or hard to get around, a recruiter can leave before seeing the work at all.
Solution
A site I designed in Figma and built in plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript: quick to load, easy to navigate on any device, with small details that make browsing easier.
Constraint
Live by the end of October, with no frameworks, and working on every screen size.

Who It's For

Recruiters first, then anyone who lands here.

The site is for recruiters, for me, and for anyone who ends up on it. A recruiter might give a portfolio less than a minute, so every page tries to get them to the essentials fast: who I am, what I've made, and how to reach me.

  • Works everywhere

    Phones, tablets, and computers. I designed every page at three sizes so it reads well on any of them.

  • Feels quick

    Pages load and change fast. Many sites I came across lag or load slowly, and I didn't want mine to be one of them.

  • Feels like me

    Simple, clean, organized, and empathetic. Plenty of portfolios lead with bold art or code-heavy visuals, but that isn't who I am.

Early Concepts

From plain, to playful, and back to clean.

I started with the simplest version of what I wanted: a streamlined, minimal portfolio that gets straight to the point. The second version is nearly the same; the only change moved the navigation links from the hero into the header.

It felt too basic, so I experimented with lined paper. Loose-leaf paper is something nearly everyone has used, it works on a light or dark background, and it's academic, organized, and to the point, much like the years of school behind it. I then set the page on my family's wooden table, a fixture in many Asian households, and turned the navigation into sticky-note tabs to make it feel real.

I dropped it because there was too much going on: the table, the paper on top of it, the different-colored tabs, and lines that made text hard to fit. Lined paper is also much taller than it is wide, so it couldn't simply stretch across a wide screen, which is why the table filled the sides.

Next, I tried a blue gradient with project cards. It was still simple, but it was clean, and I liked that. Blue is my favorite color, and it conveys trust. The second blue version got darker toward the bottom, like a sky, moved the project cards down, and let the landing page fill the whole screen.

Easy on the Eyes

A warm off-white and a soft gray, in light and dark.

In the end I dropped the blue. Not everyone likes blue, or any single color, so I'd rather the site be a little "boring" and neutral and let the work stand out. Everyone should be able to use it comfortably.

Neither theme is pure white or pure black. Pure white is harsh on the eyes, so the light theme is slightly warm, the way True Tone and Night Shift warm up a screen, without looking yellowed. The dark theme is a gray that's dark enough to make a difference but not so dark that it's hard to look at, since pure black can be harsh too.

People also have preferences: some like dark mode, some like light, and some like to switch. The site opens in whichever theme the device is set to, and a button in the header switches it.

A Small Mark

The icon in the browser tab, after 26 tries.

The favicon is the small icon in the browser tab and in bookmarks, so it has to read at just a few pixels wide. I tried 26 designs: my initials, NL, in blue; my name spelled out, on one line and stacked; a single bold N, some cropped to fill the whole square; and Ns with layered, stepped shadows, upright and turned at an angle.

From Figma to Code

Designed at three sizes, then built frame by frame.

I designed every page at three widths: 393px for phones, 768px for tablets, and 1440px for computers. I laid each frame out with absolute positioning first, which gave me full control to see how every element looked next to the others. Then I rebuilt each one with auto layout, which works much like CSS flexbox, the part of CSS I knew best. That made moving to code almost one to one.

I built the site in plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, with no frameworks: just VS Code and three files. I worked page by page, going through each component in Figma and coding it. I chose to build it myself because that's the pipeline I know, and because building it would teach me HTML, CSS, and JavaScript along the way.

In code, the breakpoints moved. The desktop layout switched at 1280px instead of 1440px, and a new breakpoint appeared at 1024px, because the gap between the tablet and desktop designs was too big. A layout that looked right at 768px looked odd at 1280px.

In hindsightI didn't plan for those in-between sizes in Figma. Designing for more widths up front would have saved reworking layouts in code.

Small Things, Less Effort

Shortcuts that save a click, everywhere on the site.

  • Home from my name

    Hovering my name in the header shows a small arrow, a hint that it leads home.

  • Projects from the menu

    Hovering Work opens a list of projects, so visitors can go straight to one without stopping at the Work page.

  • Previous and next

    The end of each project links to the next one, so there's no trip back to the Work page.

  • On this page

    A list of sections jumps anywhere in a case study and shows where you are: at the side on wide screens, in the bottom bar on smaller ones.

  • Return to top

    One click back to the top of a long page.

  • Theme toggle

    Light or dark, starting from the device's own setting.

Around those are small touches that are easy to miss: pages fade in, a line under the header fills as you scroll, the location arrow on the home page, the bouncing arrow that invites a scroll, and "User Experience Designer" fading away as the page moves. None of them get in the way, and together they make the site feel looked after.

Building with Claude Code

I designed it; Claude Code helped me build it faster.

I coded the first version by hand: the navigation, dark mode, the page fade-in, the scroll progress, the breakpoints, and the Work menu. As the site grew, so did the complexity. Some of it would have taken months to learn, and I had a deadline, so I switched to Claude Code to put changes in place faster.

I'm a designer, not a design engineer, with enough front-end knowledge to take a design live. I made the design decisions and reviewed every change before keeping it. Claude Code suggested some ideas, mostly small stylistic tweaks, but about 90% of the site is my own ideas.

I also said no often. I turned down two redesigns of the Work page, several takes on the header's glass blur, and a theme switch that spread out from the button in a circle.

I tested the site with design friends and with people outside design, since each group notices different things.

Next Steps

More case studies, and steady refinement.

The site only recently went live, so there's nothing to measure yet. Next, I'll add case studies as I finish them, starting with a refresh of the San Francisco Public Library website, and keep making small improvements as I think of them.

What I Learned

Plan for every screen size from the start, not just three.

A simple, neutral site can still feel personal. The personality lives in the small details, not in the colors.
