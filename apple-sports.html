Conceptualizing Team Schedules and Results for Apple Sports

CONCEPT INTERACTION DESIGN PROTOTYPING MOBILE
Role
Designer
Duration
Mar 2026 - Apr 2026
Tools
Figma
Team
Solo Project

Overview

Problem
Apple Sports has no way to follow a single team. Results vanish after a day, and upcoming games are buried among every other team and sport.
Solution
A team page with recent results and the upcoming schedule, opened from the places where you've already shown interest in that team.
Constraint
Add as little interface as possible. Keep what makes it Apple Sports: fast, current, and minimal.
Try the prototype View in Figma

The Problem

The main list only covers a few days in each direction. Previous stops at yesterday, and Upcoming shows the next few games mixed in with every other team. To follow one team, fans leave for ESPN, Yahoo, or theScore.

Die-hard and casual fans want different levels of depth, but they ask the same two questions: how has my team been doing, and when do they play next?

  • Monday's game, gone by Wednesday

    The Warriors played Monday. On Wednesday, the Yesterday tab only shows Tuesday's games, so their result is already gone.

  • Are they winning lately?

    There's no way to see a team's last five games anywhere in the app.

  • Is a tough stretch coming?

    Upcoming shows only the next few games, buried among other teams. Planning a week, let alone a month, is impossible.

It's sharper in weekly sports. In the NFL, the Yesterday tab is empty most days.

The Solution

Turn Yesterday into Previous, which shows the most recent games, and give every team its own page.

A team page opens from two places: a team's logo in a game's card, and the team's name in the league menu. Both are moments when you've already shown interest in that team. It opens inside the existing slide-up card, so it adds one layer and nothing about the main app changes.

Where It Lives

Two entry points, one layer deep. Not three, and not one.

I considered the alternatives on either side and rejected both.

  • Tap a logo in the main list

    A third entry point, and it shrinks the area you tap to open the game card.

  • Game card only, no menu entry

    You could only reach your team on days it appears in the list. The teams were already in the menu, so I gave them somewhere to go.

What I Left Out

Rosters, news, and stats.

Each would bloat a page built to be quick. Schedules and results are different: they aren't new content, just the games the app already shows, filtered to one team. That makes them a core function, not an add-on.

In

  • Recent results
  • Upcoming schedule
  • Where to watch

Out

  • Rosters
  • News
  • Stats

The Hardest Decision

Breaking the app's game row to fit the date and the network.

Apple Sports shows a game as team, record, time, record, team, with the date above. I needed to add the date and the TV network, and squeezing both into that row made it cluttered.

The network drove the change. With games spread across ESPN, Prime Video, Peacock, and more, knowing where to watch is genuinely hard, and seeing the network shows at a glance which games are on national TV. I shortened long network names to keep the rows clean.

TradeoffThese rows look different from the rest of the app, but they keep its clean, minimal style. It's different, not foreign.

Making It Feel Native

An extension of the app, not a new screen.

Motion
Built inside the existing slide-up card, with the team's logo flying into place.
Type
SF Pro throughout, with SF Pro Rounded only for times, as Apple Sports does.
Color
Layered gradients in each team's colors add depth, in the style of Apple's own backgrounds.
Materials
Apple's iOS design resources in Figma for the see-through, blurred materials.
Spacing
Measured from the existing app, then held to one scale.

Measuring Success

Usage
How often team pages are opened.
Return
Whether people come back to them.
Retention
Whether people who follow one team keep using the app over time.

Biggest riskDiscoverability. Apple tends to keep features out of sight. A one-time tip when the app opens, like the built-in tips iOS already uses, would point out the feature without cluttering the interface.

What I Learned

The feature was simple. The hard part was deciding where it lives, what it leaves out, and how far to move from the existing design.

A small, well-placed addition can solve a real problem without changing what makes a product good.
